Understanding Plant Families

Plant families group plants that share evolutionary and physical characteristics. Explore four important families and discover the traits that help distinguish them.

A plant family is the taxonomic rank that groups together genera sharing a common evolutionary ancestor and a broadly similar flower and fruit structure. Forest Story features four families — two rooted mainly in tropical Asia, and two whose genera split between Asia, Africa and the Americas. Each family entry below follows the same structure: first its typical growth habit and distribution, then its most diagnostic leaf, flower and fruit traits, and finally the two genera profiled on this site. Read the Genera and Species pages for finer detail on the plants within each family.

Myristicaceae — the Nutmeg Family

Myristicaceae is a family of aromatic evergreen trees found across the tropics of Asia, Africa and the Americas, most famous for giving the world commercial nutmeg. Members typically have alternate, simple, leathery leaves and small, inconspicuous, single-sexed flowers, since individual trees are either male or female. The signature trait of the family is its fruit: a fleshy capsule that splits open to reveal a single seed wrapped in a bright red or orange aril, a structure prized by hornbills and pigeons as much as by cooks. Our two featured genera, Horsfieldia and Knema, are both large, species-rich genera concentrated in the rainforests of tropical Asia.

Genera in this family

  • Horsfieldia — trees ranging from the eastern Himalaya to Sumatra and the Malay Peninsula.
  • Knema — trees of central and eastern Malesia with densely hairy fruit.

Chrysobalanaceae — the Cocoplum Family

Chrysobalanaceae is a pantropical family whose genera are split between Asia and the Neotropics — a pattern reflected directly in our two chosen genera. Species are trees or shrubs with alternate, simple leaves, small paired stipules at the leaf base, and small white, cream or pink flowers arranged in racemes or panicles. The fruit is typically a single-seeded drupe, often edible, that gives the family its common name. Parastemon represents the family's Southeast Asian, peat-swamp-forest lineage, while Hirtella represents its Central and South American, rainforest-understorey lineage.

Genera in this family

  • Parastemon — large canopy trees of Southeast Asian peat-swamp and heath forest.
  • Hirtella — shrubs and small understorey trees of Central and South America.

Meliaceae — the Mahogany Family

Meliaceae is the family that gives the timber industry true mahogany, and its two featured genera here, Aglaia and Dysoxylum, are large Asian-Pacific genera valued for both timber and fruit. Unlike the previous two families, most Meliaceae species carry pinnately compound leaves rather than simple ones, often covered in distinctive star-shaped (stellate) or scaly hairs. Flowers are small but frequently intensely fragrant, and fruit is usually a capsule or berry containing seeds covered in an edible, oily aril. Several species in this family are important canopy or emergent rainforest trees reaching well over 30 metres tall.

Genera in this family

  • Aglaia — trees and shrubs known for fragrant flowers and edible arillate fruit.
  • Dysoxylum — large timber trees ranging from mainland Asia to northern Australia.

Ebenaceae — the Ebony Family

Ebenaceae is best known for producing the dense, jet-black heartwood traditionally called ebony. Like Chrysobalanaceae, this family's genera are split across continents: Diospyros (which also gives us the cultivated persimmon) is enormously diverse across Asia, while Euclea is its African counterpart. Both genera share simple, alternate, often leathery leaves, separate male and female flowers, and a berry-type fruit seated in a persistent, often enlarged calyx. Several species are important sources of traditional medicine as well as fine timber.

Genera in this family

  • Diospyros — the ebony genus, ranging from India across Malesia to the Pacific.
  • Euclea — shrubs and small trees native to eastern and southern Africa.

Why families matter

Taxonomic families are more than filing categories — plants in the same family often share useful chemical compounds, pollinator relationships, and vulnerabilities to pests or disease. Foresters and conservationists frequently make management decisions at the family level when species-specific data is unavailable.
