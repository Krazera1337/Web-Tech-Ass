Horsfieldia flocculosa

Family
Myristicaceae
Genus
Horsfieldia
Common name(s)
Not available
Plant type
Tree
Distribution
Native — Peninsular Malaysia, Sumatra (Indonesia)
Conservation status
Vulnerable (IUCN)

Horsfieldia flocculosa is an evergreen tree first described in 1891 as Myristica flocculosa by George King, later transferred to Horsfieldia by Otto Warburg. It is a lowland rainforest tree restricted to the western Malay Peninsula (Pahang, Negeri Sembilan, Selangor, Johor) and Sumatra, growing in lowland tropical rainforest. As with other Myristicaceae, it likely produces reddish sap and small, unisexual flowers typical of the family.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Species-specific measurements were not found in accessible literature; family members typically have simple, alternate, entire leaves, small unisexual flowers with fused inconspicuous tepals, and fruit that splits along a single suture to reveal a seed covered by a brightly coloured aril.

Ecology & uses: Likely provides aril-covered fruit attractive to birds such as hornbills and pigeons, aiding seed dispersal, though this has not been specifically documented for this species. No documented traditional or economic uses were found. Its Vulnerable IUCN rating reflects ongoing loss of its restricted rainforest habitat.

  1. Originally described as Myristica flocculosa in 1891, later moved to Horsfieldia.
  2. Endemic to a small area of the western Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, making it vulnerable to deforestation.

Horsfieldia kingii

Family
Myristicaceae
Genus
Horsfieldia
Common name(s)
Ramtamul (Assamese)
Plant type
Tree
Distribution
Native — Nepal, India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam, China
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

Horsfieldia kingii is a dioecious evergreen tree found across the eastern Himalayan foothills and mainland Southeast Asia, ranging from eastern Nepal through northeastern India, Bangladesh, Myanmar, northern Thailand and Vietnam to southern China. First described as Myristica kingii by J.D. Hooker in 1886, it was later transferred to Horsfieldia by Otto Warburg.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves obovate to oblanceolate, 12–55 x 5–15 cm, papery, glabrous, with 14–18 pairs of lateral veins. Male and female flowers occur on separate trees; female buds are nearly globose, about 5 mm. Fruit is an ovoid-to-oblong arillate capsule, 4–4.5 cm long, with a thick, leathery, bi-coloured pericarp, fruiting February–May.

Ecology & uses: Fruits are a key food source for hornbills and imperial pigeons, which disperse the large seeds; foliage is browsed by the endangered capped langur. The fruit/seed is sometimes used as a substitute for betel nut.

  1. First described as Myristica kingii in 1886, reclassified into Horsfieldia in 1897.
  2. Its foliage is browsed by the endangered capped langur in reserves such as Pakke Tiger Reserve.
  3. Known locally in Assamese as "Ramtamul".

Knema cinerea

Family
Myristicaceae
Genus
Knema
Common name(s)
Not available
Plant type
Tree
Distribution
Native — Indonesia (Lesser Sundas, Sulawesi, Moluccas), Philippines
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

Knema cinerea is a small to medium tree native to central and eastern Malesia, found in the Lesser Sunda Islands, Sulawesi, the Moluccas and a single collection from the Philippines. It is closely related to K. stenophylla, K. patentinervia and K. glauca, and several infraspecific varieties are recognised across its range.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves membranous to chartaceous, elliptic to oblong-lanceolate, 8–25 x 2.5–9 cm, with minute persistent scale-like or stellate hairs on the lower surface. Flowers are small and unpedicellate detail is limited. Fruit is subglobose to obovoid, 1.2–1.8 x 1–1.6 cm, covered in persistent short hairs.

Ecology & uses: Specific ecological role not documented; the genus generally provides arillate fruit eaten by birds. No documented traditional or economic uses were found.

  1. Several infraspecific varieties are recognised, including var. glauca, var. andamanica and var. sumatrana.
  2. Its lower leaf surface is covered in tiny scale-like hairs typical of the genus.

Knema sumatrana

Family
Myristicaceae
Genus
Knema
Common name(s)
Not available
Plant type

Native — Thailand, Peninsular Malaysia, Indonesia (Sumatra)
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

Knema sumatrana is a medium-sized tree (10–20 m) restricted to peninsular Thailand, Peninsular Malaysia (Perak, Kelantan) and Sumatra, including Simeulue Island. It was originally described as Myristica sumatrana by Blume before being placed in Knema by W.J.J.O. de Wilde.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves membranous to chartaceous, elliptic to oblong-lanceolate, 12–30 x 4.5–12 cm, drying greenish to brown above. Flowers are small and unisexual, as typical for the dioecious genus. Fruits are ovoid-ellipsoid to ellipsoid-oblong, 3–4 x 1.4–2 cm, with a fine indumentum lost early, leaving a finely granulate surface.

Ecology & uses: Specific ecological role and documented uses were not found in accessible sources.

  1. Originally named Myristica sumatrana by Blume in 1837.
  2. Grows 10–20 m tall, a medium-sized tree for the genus.

Family Chrysobalanaceae

Parastemon urophyllus

Family
Chrysobalanaceae
Genus
Parastemon
Common name(s)
Nyilas Padang, Malas
Plant type
Tree
Distribution
Native — India (Andaman & Nicobar), Myanmar, Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Brunei
Conservation status
Not available (no global IUCN assessment found)

Parastemon urophyllus is a large evergreen tree reaching 35–40 m tall with a long, straight, often buttressed bole. Its specific epithet "urophyllus" means "tail-leaf", referring to its tapering leaf tip. It is a common component of peat-swamp, kerangas (heath) and secondary forests across western Malesia.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves coriaceous, elliptic-lanceolate, 5–8 x 3–4 cm, with an acuminate, tail-like tip. Flowers are small (about 1.5 mm), polygamo-dioecious, borne in axillary racemes 4–14 cm long, with 5 petals, 2 fertile stamens and 3 sterile staminodes. Fruit is a smooth, yellowish, ellipsoid drupe about 12 x 6 mm.

Ecology & uses: A structurally important canopy tree of Sundaland's peat-swamp and heath forest ecosystems. Its wood is hard and durable but difficult to work due to high silica content, used locally for construction, posts and firewood.

  1. "Urophyllus" is Greek for "tail-leaf", describing its distinctive tapering leaf tip.
  2. Known by the Malay/Iban name "Nyilas Padang" and trade name "Malas".
  3. Can grow up to 40 m tall with a bole branch-free for 20 m.

Parastemon grandifructus

Parastemon grandifructus Prance is a tree endemic to Malaysian Borneo, closely related to the more widespread P. urophyllus. Its epithet "grandifructus" (Latin for "large fruit") refers to its notably larger fruit compared to its congener. It was described by botanist Ghillean Prance in 1987.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Detailed leaf and flower measurements were not found in accessible literature. Fruit is ellipsoid and notably large for the genus, up to about 3.5 cm long.

Ecology & uses: Grows in lowland forest, including swamp forest, from sea level to about 150 m. Specific ecological role and documented uses were not found.

  1. Endemic to Borneo, found only in Sarawak, Sabah and Brunei.
  2. Described by botanist Ghillean Prance in 1987.
  3. Has the largest fruit in the genus Parastemon.

Hirtella triandra

Family
Chrysobalanaceae
Genus
Hirtella
Common name(s)
Pigeonberry
Plant type
Shrub or small tree
Distribution
Native — Mexico through Central America to Brazil, Bolivia, Caribbean
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

Hirtella triandra is a shrub or tree, usually small but capable of reaching up to 15 m, native to lowland humid forests from Mexico through Central America to the Brazilian Amazon. It bears white, aromatic flowers with striking purple stamens, and its soft, sweet, purple fruit is eaten by monkeys and birds.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves simple and alternate. Flowers are borne in terminal and axillary panicles 3.5–17 cm long; white, aromatic, with 5 glabrous petals and 3 conspicuously purple stamens. Fruit is ellipsoid, purple when ripe, soft and sweet, with a thin fleshy mesocarp and a hard, hairy-lined endocarp.

Ecology & uses: Its sweet purple fruit is consumed and dispersed by monkeys and birds. Fruit is harvested from the wild for local food use.

  1. Has three recognised subspecies (subsp. triandra, media, punctulata).
  2. Its purple stamens contrast strikingly against white, aromatic flowers.

Hirtella racemosa

Family
Chrysobalanaceae
Genus
Hirtella
Common name(s)
Azeitona-da-mata (Brazil)
Plant type
Shrub or small tree
Distribution
Native — Mexico through Central America, Caribbean, to Peru, Bolivia, Brazil
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

Hirtella racemosa is a small evergreen understorey shrub or treelet, usually only 4–5 m tall, widespread from Mexico to Brazil and Bolivia. It is inconspicuous except when in bloom, when it is decorated with silky racemes of pink to lavender flowers, and it is a common pioneer species in disturbed lowland forest.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves simple, alternate, small (about 8 x 2.5 cm) and nearly sessile. Flowers are borne in silky racemes 3.5–6 mm long with 5 pink-to-lavender petals. Fruit is an elongated berry about 2 cm long, ripening from green to maroon or black, enclosing a single rod-shaped seed.

Ecology & uses: A common understorey pioneer species that helps stabilise disturbed lowland forest sites. Fruit is harvested from the wild for local food, and flexible branches are traditionally used in roof construction.

  1. Flexible branches are traditionally used to build roofs in parts of Central America.
  2. Its tiny, alternately arranged leaves make it identifiable at a glance despite its small stature.

Aglaia elliptica

Family
Meliaceae
Genus
Aglaia
Common name(s)
Lambunau (Malaysia)
Plant type
Tree
Distribution
Native — Myanmar, Thailand, Malaysia, Brunei, Indonesia, Philippines
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

Aglaia elliptica is a tree most often found along riverbanks and in forest subject to periodic flooding, from Indo-China through western and central Malesia. It bears orange, ellipsoid fruit with an edible, cranberry-like flavoured aril, and two subspecies are recognised: subsp. elliptica (widespread) and subsp. clementis (Borneo, Sulawesi).

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves compound with several leaflets, bearing stellate hairs or scales only (no peltate scales, unlike some related species). Fruit is orange, ellipsoid, up to 35 x 30 mm, usually with a longitudinal ridge and a dense reddish-brown indumentum; the aril has a cranberry-like taste.

Ecology & uses: Its edible, aril-covered fruit likely attracts birds and other frugivores that aid seed dispersal. In the Philippines (Mindanao), a boiled bark decoction is used as a bath against tumours, and leaves are applied to wounds; wood is also harvested.

  1. Its fruit has a cranberry-like taste and is eaten locally.
  2. Distinguished from related Aglaia by lacking peltate leaf scales.
  3. Traditional Philippine medicine uses its bark decoction against tumours.

Aglaia korthalsii

Family
Meliaceae
Genus
Aglaia
Common name(s)
Keriat / Sekiat (Malay)
Plant type
Tree
Distribution
Native — Bhutan, India (Nicobar Is.), Myanmar, Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia
Conservation status
Near Threatened (older 1998 IUCN assessment — treat as potentially outdated)

Aglaia korthalsii is a small to medium dioecious tree ranging from Bhutan through mainland Southeast Asia to western Malesia. It is notable for its intensely fragrant, rice-grain-sized yellow flowers and orange, edible fruit, which was historically cultivated as a village fruit tree in parts of Peninsular Malaysia.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves odd-pinnate, up to 30 cm long, usually with 5 leaflets covered in shiny reddish-brown scales. Flowers are tiny, yellow and intensely fragrant. Fruit is ellipsoid to subglobose, 20–40 mm long, orange when ripe, with up to 3 edible seeds and notably high vitamin C content.

Ecology & uses: Fruit is edible and historically grown as a village fruit tree in Kelantan and Terengganu; wood is used for house poles. The species contains flavagline compounds under research for insecticidal, antifungal, antibacterial and anticancer properties.

  1. Its tiny yellow flowers, each about the size of a rice grain, produce an unusually strong fragrance.
  2. Traditionally cultivated as a village fruit tree ("keriat") in Kelantan and Terengganu.
  3. Contains compounds being studied for anticancer and antimicrobial properties.

Dysoxylum acutangulum

Family
Meliaceae
Genus
Dysoxylum
Common name(s)
Membalo (trade name), Bekak (Malaysia), Langkang (Borneo)
Plant type
Tree
Distribution
Native — Thailand to Malesia, Solomon Islands, northern Australia
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

Dysoxylum acutangulum is a massive upper-canopy timber tree found in rainforests from Thailand and Malesia to northern Australia. Known commercially as "membalo", it is one of the most important timber trees in Meliaceae across northern Malesia, valued for furniture, coffins and construction.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves paripinnate, with a rachis about 30 cm long bearing about four pairs of elliptical leaflets. Inflorescence is a cauliflorous panicle (borne on the trunk) about 10 cm long, with four yellowish petals. Fruit is a four-valved, orange capsule with black seeds.

Ecology & uses: As a large emergent canopy tree, it contributes significantly to rainforest structure. Its wood is used for furniture, Chinese coffins and construction; in Indonesia, its seeds are used to poison fish.

  1. Can reach 47 m tall with a bole branchless for up to 18 m — among the largest trees in its family.
  2. Its seeds are traditionally used as a fish poison in Indonesia.

Dysoxylum grande Hiern is a large tree found from Bhutan through southern China, Thailand and mainland Southeast Asia to western and central Malesia. It bears fragrant, creamy-yellow flowers and large, round, orange fruit up to 11 cm in diameter — among the largest fruit in the genus.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Detailed leaf measurements are not available for this species specifically; related Dysoxylum have large, odd-pinnate compound leaves. Flowers are fragrant and creamy-yellow. Fruit is orange, roundish, up to about 11 cm across.

Ecology & uses: Its large, orange fruit likely provides food for large frugivores across its wide range, though this has not been specifically documented. No species-specific traditional or economic uses were found.

  1. Grows up to 39 m tall with a trunk diameter of up to 70 cm.
  2. Produces some of the largest fruit in the genus Dysoxylum, up to 11 cm across.

Diospyros ferrea

Family
Ebenaceae
Genus
Diospyros
Common name(s)
Black ebony, English ironwood
Plant type
Shrub or small to medium tree
Distribution
Native — India, Sri Lanka to Melanesia, Micronesia, Polynesia, Hawaii
Conservation status
Not available (no current global IUCN assessment found)

Diospyros ferrea is a variable evergreen shrub or small tree distributed extremely widely from South and Southeast Asia across Malesia to Melanesia, Micronesia, Polynesia and Hawaii — one of the most geographically widespread species in the genus. It has small, leathery leaves and small yellow-brown fruit.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves alternate, small (1–2.5 x 1–1.8 cm), obovate-oblong to spathulate, coriaceous, with a very short petiole. Flowers are white. Fruit is a subglobose berry 5–7 mm long, yellow to brownish-red when ripe, containing 1–3 wedge-shaped seeds.

Ecology & uses: As a widespread understorey/coastal shrub-tree, it likely supports local frugivores. In traditional medicine, leaves are used for lumbago, fruit is considered carminative and astringent (Ayurveda), seeds are used as a sedative, and bark is used as a bitter, astringent febrifuge.

  1. One of the most geographically widespread Diospyros species, ranging from India to Hawaii.
  2. Belongs to the ebony genus, though its wood is not commercially significant like D. ebenum.
  3. Highly variable across its range, with several taxonomic varieties described.

Diospyros ebenum

Family
Ebenaceae
Genus
Diospyros
Common name(s)
Ceylon ebony, East Indian ebony, True ebony
Plant type
Tree
Distribution
Native — southern India, Sri Lanka
Conservation status
Data Deficient (IUCN)

Diospyros ebenum is a slow-growing evergreen tree of southern India and Sri Lanka, historically the primary commercial source of the finest jet-black ebony timber. Its heartwood is dense, glossy black and famed for use in fine furniture, musical instruments and carved objects, which has led to overexploitation across much of its natural range.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves dark green and glossy, elliptic-lanceolate to ovate, 6–15 x 2–5 cm. Male flowers occur in 3–16-flowered cymes with 16 stamens; female and bisexual flowers are solitary. Fruit is a depressed-globose berry up to 1.5 cm across.

Ecology & uses: A slow-growing forest tree of seasonally dry tropical forest; its astringent fruit has been used as a famine food and fish poison. Its wood is the classic source of finest commercial black ebony, used for sports goods, musical instruments, piano keys, chess pieces and turnery. Heartwood has a specific gravity of about 1.12 — dense enough to sink in water.

  1. The historical source of the finest, jet-black commercial ebony wood.
  2. Its heartwood is dense enough to sink in water.
  3. Listed as Data Deficient by IUCN despite historical commercial importance, reflecting a lack of recent population assessment.

Euclea natalensis

Family
Ebenaceae
Genus
Euclea
Common name(s)
Natal guarri
Plant type
Shrub or small to medium tree
Distribution
Native — Ethiopia, Somalia south through East Africa to South Africa
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

The Natal guarri, Euclea natalensis, is a shrub or small tree native to eastern and southern Africa, from Ethiopia and Somalia south to South Africa's Western Cape. It has hard, glossy, leathery leaves often covered in reddish hairs beneath, and it is a well-known traditional medicinal plant across tropical Africa.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves hard and leathery, dark green and shiny above, often with thick, velvety red hairs beneath. Flowers are small, greenish-cream to pale yellow and scented, borne in axillary clusters of 10–30. Fruit is a globose berry, red ripening to black, edible.

Ecology & uses: Its edible berries are an important food source for birds, monkeys and people. Twigs are traditionally used as chewing-stick toothbrushes, and root/bark infusions are used across tropical Africa to treat intestinal worms, stomach disorders, chest complaints, headache and other ailments.

  1. One of the most important traditional medicinal plants in tropical Africa.
  2. Its twigs are used as natural toothbrushes ("chewing sticks").
  3. Under warm, well-watered conditions can grow into a medium tree 12–18 m tall.

Euclea racemosa

Family
Ebenaceae
Genus
Euclea
Common name(s)
Sea guarrie, Dune guarrie
Plant type
Shrub or small tree
Distribution
Native — Egypt, Arabian Peninsula, East Africa to South Africa, Comoros
Conservation status
Least Concern (IUCN)

The sea guarrie, Euclea racemosa, is an evergreen shrub or small tree widespread along coasts and in scrub from Egypt and the Arabian Peninsula south through East Africa to South Africa's Cape, and on Comoros. It bears sweetly scented, drooping cream-white flowers, and its fine, heavy, reddish-purple timber has long been used for tool handles and turnery.

Leaf, flower & fruit: Leaves thickly leathery, oblanceolate to obovate, up to 11 x 4 cm, dark glossy green above. Flowers are small, creamy-white and drooping, borne in 8–20-flowered axillary spikes, blooming December–March. Fruit is round, 5–7 mm across, ripening black.

Ecology & uses: Its black berries provide food for birds and wildlife along coastal habitats, and the shrub helps stabilise coastal scrub and dune vegetation. Its fine-grained, heavy timber has been used by wheelwrights and turners for wooden screws and tool parts.

  1. Commonly called the "sea guarrie" or "dune guarrie" for its typical coastal habitat.
  2. Its sweetly scented flowers bloom in the southern hemisphere summer (December–March).
  3. Its dense, reddish-purple wood was traditionally prized by wheelwrights.
